01 · The brief

One question:
who are the members?

A large national gym chain wants to understand its members. The brief: find distinct segments in the membership details of 2,000 members, describe them for management, and recommend marketing for each segment, using k-means++ and agglomerative clustering on all seven fields.

I treated two clusters as the start, not the answer. A segment earns its place if it is stable, explained by fields a club can see, and different enough to change what the club does.

Who a segmentation serves

MarketingWhich offer and plan each member hears about first.
Club managersWhich class formats and hours each club runs.
Product & pricingWhich membership tiers to sell, and where.
Data & CRMWhat to ask at sign-up, and what to stop asking.

What made the data tricky

  • One axisIncome follows age almost exactly (r = 0.9995), and marital status, job, education and city move together. Seven fields carry about one dimension of information.
  • Mixed typesTwo yes-or-no fields, three coded categories and dollars share one distance scale. The brief standardises only age and income, so one step in education counts as much as nine and a half years of age.
  • SensitiveTwo of the seven fields are gender and marital status, which members may not expect a gym to use for targeting.

03 · Method

The brief's method,
then six ways to break it.

I ran the clustering exactly as briefed, then changed one assumption at a time to see whether the segments survived.

01

Check

Read the fields first.

  • Missing values and duplicates
  • Income against age
  • Rank correlations between all seven fields
02

Scale

Standardise age and income.

  • Z-scores for age and income, as the brief requires
  • Coded fields keep their 0–1 to 0–3 codes
  • No dimensionality reduction
03

Choose k

Elbow and silhouette.

  • Inertia for k = 1 to 10
  • Silhouette for k = 2 to 10
  • k = 2 scores 0.545
04

Cluster

Two methods, all seven fields.

  • K-means++, 10 starts, fixed seed
  • Agglomerative clustering, Ward linkage
  • Segments described in proportions
05

Stress-test

Change one thing at a time.

  • 30 random starts; average and complete linkage
  • All fields standardised; without income
  • Without gender and marital status; Gower distance
Core clustering · deep_dive.py 
X = df[FIELDS].astype(float)
X[['Age', 'Income']] = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X[['Age', 'Income']])
# ↑ the brief: standardise the two numeric fields only
km = KMeans(n_clusters=2, init='k-means++', n_init=10,
            random_state=0).fit_predict(X)
ward = AgglomerativeClustering(n_clusters=2,
            linkage='ward').fit_predict(X)
df['silhouette'] = silhouette_samples(X, km)
# members below 0.25 sit almost as close to the other segment
What counts as the same segment
  • Agreement: the share of members another method puts in the same segment, after matching labels
  • Adjusted Rand index as a second measure, which corrects for chance
  • Silhouette per member: how much closer a member is to their own segment than to the other
  • Every check reruns the full method, not a sample

The original notebook's agglomerative cell used complete linkage, while the report's table matched Ward, scikit-learn's default. The notebook in the repository now uses Ward and a fixed k-means seed, so it reproduces the report: k-means 1,024 and 976 members, Ward 992 and 1,008.

04 · What the data says

Six findings on
who the members are.

Segments

K-means++ on all seven fields with age and income standardised, k = 2. Named by life stage: Starters and Established.

Agreement

The share of members that another method or encoding puts in the same segment, after matching labels.

Read with care

Seven membership fields and no behaviour: the data says who members are, not how often they come, what they pay or who leaves.
01

The data has one axis, not seven.

Income follows age almost exactly: income ≈ −$61,784 + $4,882 × age (r = 0.9995), so age alone predicts a member's income with a typical error of about $1,400. Marital status moves with job type (rank correlation 0.95), education (0.88) and city size (0.79).

So the clustering finds the two ends of one line: younger, single, school-educated members in unskilled work in smaller cities, and older, non-single, university-educated professionals in big cities.

So whatSeven fields ask one question: how far along in life is this member? Two members at the same point on that line look identical to this data, however differently they use the gym.
02

Two segments, and every method agrees.

Two segments score a silhouette of 0.545, against 0.433 for three and 0.452 for four. Thirty random starts return the same split every time.

Ward linkage puts 97.9% of members in the same segment as k-means, average linkage 98.8% and Gower distance 98.0%. Only complete linkage, which judges clusters by their farthest members, drifts to 90.7%.

So whatThe split is real for this data: a team that reruns it gets the same two groups. Agreement alone doesn't make them useful, though; finding 01 says why.
03

Two profiles, in proportions.

Starters (1,024, 51%): mean age 34, most aged 27–41; 97% single, 63.5% male; 97% with a high-school or other education; 96% unemployed or in unskilled work; 93% in small cities and none in big ones. Median income $102,356.

Established (976, 49%): mean age 48, most aged 40–57; 99% non-single (married, separated, divorced or widowed), 85.6% female; 93% university-educated; 99% in skilled or management roles; 82% in big cities. Median income $171,798, and 403 of them (41%) are 50 or older.

So whatNames should follow the numbers. My first report called Starters "young, single males", but 37% are women; and calling Established members "urban" misses the 178 who live in small cities.
04

Where members live already sorts them.

All 798 big-city members are Established, and all 72 mid-sized-city members are Starters. Small cities hold both: 952 Starters (84%) and 178 Established members (16%).

Those 178 look like big-city Established members in everything but address: mean age 49, median income $175,545, 97% in skilled or management work and 90% university-educated.

So whatIf members train near home, a club's city predicts most of its members' segment before anyone fills in a form. Set formats by club, and keep a premium option where the 16% live.
05

No need to ask gender or marital status.

Clustering without gender and marital status keeps 99.4% of members in the same segment. Marital status alone matches the split for 98.0% of members, but job type alone matches 97.7%, and a two-part rule (big city, or a skilled or management job, means Established) matches 97.8%.

Gender is the weakest guide: on its own it reproduces only 74% of the split. Starters are 36.5% female and Established members 85.6%.

So whatSegment on fields members expect a gym to use, such as where they live and the kind of work they do. Gender and marital status add almost nothing and carry the most risk.
06

One member in fifteen sits in between.

134 members (6.7%) score a silhouette below 0.25: they are almost as close to the other segment as to their own. Most are non-single (88%), in skilled or management work (88%) and university-educated (77%), aged 28–59, and three in four live in small cities.

73 of them are small-city Established members from finding 04: by job and education they're Established, by address they're Starters.

So whatTwo fixed offers will misfire for this group. Let members choose from a plan menu instead of guessing their segment.

05 · Short-term actions, next 0–6 months

What each team
can do now.

The segments differ most in life stage, job and city. Offers should follow those, and members on the boundary should choose for themselves.

01

Lead with flexible plans for Starters

Do

Promote month-to-month and off-peak plans with no joining fee in small and mid-sized city clubs.

Why

Starters are 1,024 members (51%): 96% are unemployed or in unskilled work, and their median income ($102K) is 40% below Established members' ($172K).

Track90-day retention of new Starters
02

Sell employer and premium plans to Established

Do

Sign corporate wellness agreements with big-city employers, and add a premium tier with booked express sessions.

Why

99% of Established members work in skilled or management roles, and 82% live in big cities.

TrackMembers joining through employer agreements
03

Let the in-between members choose

Do

Show every new member the same three tiers (flex, standard and premium) and let them pick, instead of pushing one segment's offer.

Why

134 members (6.7%) sit between the segments; 73 are Established members in small cities who would otherwise get Starter offers.

TrackTier mix and upgrades by segment

Location sorts the members before marketing does: every big-city member is Established, and 84% of small-city members are Starters.

04

Set each club's format by its city

Do

Run the Established format (lunchtime and after-work express classes, premium tier) in big-city clubs, and the Starter format (flexible plans, community classes) in small and mid-sized city clubs.

Why

All 798 big-city members are Established; all 72 mid-sized-city members and 952 of 1,130 small-city members are Starters.

TrackJoins and 90-day retention by club format
05

Keep a premium option in small-city clubs

Do

Carry the premium tier and an evening class block in small-city clubs too.

Why

178 Established members (16% of small-city members) live there: mean age 49, median income $175,545, 97% in skilled or management work.

TrackPremium take-up in small-city clubs
06

Programme for members over 50

Do

Add strength, mobility and low-impact classes at off-peak times in big-city clubs.

Why

403 Established members (41%) are 50 or older and 54 are 60 or older; Starters include just one member aged 50 or over.

TrackClass fill rate and over-50 retention

The membership details hold seven fields that describe one life stage. Ask for fewer, and start recording what members do.

07

Drop income from the sign-up form

Do

Stop asking members for their income, and let the plan they choose signal what they're willing to pay.

Why

Income follows age almost exactly (r = 0.9995), so it adds almost nothing that age doesn't already say, and it's one of the most personal questions a member is asked.

TrackSign-up completion rate
08

Assign segments without gender or marital status

Do

Assign each member a segment from city and job type: big city, or a skilled or management job, means Established; otherwise Starter.

Why

The rule matches the clustering for 97.8% of members, and dropping gender and marital status keeps 99.4% in the same segment.

TrackRule against a quarterly re-cluster
09

Start recording behaviour

Do

Log visits per week, class bookings, plan, tenure and the reason for every cancellation.

Why

All seven fields describe one life-stage axis; none says who visits, who leaves or what they'd pay for.

TrackMembers with 90 days of visit data

What carries over

Read the fields before the clusters

Income repeated age (r = 0.9995). Spotting that first explains why every method agreed.

Report proportions, not modes

A cluster labelled "male" was 36.5% female. Percentages keep segment names honest.

Agreement isn't insight

Methods agree when the data has one axis. Whether a segment matters depends on behaviour this data doesn't have.

Ask for less

The segments held for 99.4% of members without gender or marital status.

What I'd change next time

  • Use a distance built for mixed data, such as Gower or k-prototypes, from the start.
  • Judge segments by an outcome, such as retention or visits, not by silhouette alone.
  • Collect behaviour first, then test offers against a control group before rolling them out.
  • Re-run the segmentation on each quarter's new members to see whether it holds.
